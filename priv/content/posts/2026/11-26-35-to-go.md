In [A notebook for every page](/posts/a-notebook-for-every-page) I mentioned that every example Giggly can't draw yet says so in the same words, "Not in Giggly yet", so the notebooks double as a to-do list. A to-do list is also a scoreboard, so let's check the score.

There are 644 examples across the 141 notebooks. Giggly 0.2 went out yesterday, and it draws 568 of them. Six weeks ago it drew 26.

## Six weeks of drawing

All the notebooks were written in one go on October 15, with ggplot2's code for every example and Giggly's for the handful it could already draw. Everything since then is Giggly catching up:

![Fig. 1 — Examples Giggly draws, at the end of each day with a change to the notebooks.](/charts/2026-11-26-35-to-go/drawn.svg)

The best day was November 4, with 54 examples. That's not one big feature, it's a lot of small ones landing at once, because examples tend to wait on several things at a time.

Then look at the middle of November. From November 8 to 20 the line barely moves: 27 examples in twelve days. I had a whole paragraph written about the long tail, the easy wins being gone and everything left needing something big or something weird. Then it shot back up, 68 examples in four days. So much for the long tail.

What actually happened is less dramatic. The first few of those days went into options for things Giggly already drew, like every one of `coord_radial()`'s and the facets', which finish a handful of examples at a time. Then Giggly's insides got rebuilt so that each geom and stat describes itself, and that turned into a plugin API, so a package like [gig_ridges](https://hex.pm/packages/gig_ridges) can bring geoms and stats of its own. And then Giggly made friends: [kino_giggly](https://hex.pm/packages/kino_giggly), which draws plots in Livebook, and [gig_datasets](https://hex.pm/packages/gig_datasets), a package of their own for the datasets. None of that draws an example in ggplot2's reference, so the count sat still while the village grew.

## Counting before building

Here's the part I like. Since every waiting example names what it's waiting for, picking the next thing to build isn't a matter of taste. You count.

Early on, on October 16, color was the obvious winner: 283 of the 619 waiting examples needed color or fill. But only six of them needed nothing else. Most were also waiting on jitter, a smooth, `labs()` or a scale function. So the count has to answer two questions: how many examples name a feature, and how many it actually finishes.

Every increment after that started the same way, with the count written into Giggly's design notes:

- **Text and titles** came next. Titles were named by 35 examples, second only to expressions in a mapping.
- **Scale functions** were Giggly's thinnest part at the time: 125 examples named one, and 30 needed nothing else.
- **Expressions in a mapping**, like `factor(cyl)` or `after_stat(count)`, were the most named thing of all, in 119 examples.
- **Size, shape and line type** were named by 86 examples, more than any other candidate.
- **The theme**: 25 examples needed nothing else.

Claude does the counting, which it is very good at, and then proposes the next increment, and I agree or don't. It's the most data-driven roadmap I've ever had, which is fitting for a charting library. It's also hard to argue with yourself when the numbers are right there.

## Where the 126 went

On November 13, 126 examples were waiting. Here's where each of them stands now, grouped by what it was waiting on then:

![Fig. 2 — The 126 examples waiting on November 13, by what they waited on, and where each one stands in Giggly 0.2.](/charts/2026-11-26-35-to-go/fate.svg)

- **Stat plumbing** (20), **theme palettes** (9) and **functions in a mapping** (8) are all drawn. That's `stat_manual()`, `after_scale()` and `stage()`, a theme setting the default palettes and `element_geom()`, and functions that see a whole column at once, like `max(count)`.
- **The `movies` dataset** (10) is all drawn too. It's in gig_datasets now, nearly 60,000 films of it. Ten examples for one dataset was too good a deal to pass up.
- **Statistical models** (11): six are drawn, including the lines from `lm()` and quantile regression. The other five are set aside. Two fit a logistic regression with `glm()`, and Giggly leaves the modelling to you by design: fit your curve and draw it with `geom_function()`. Two summarize R's random numbers, which would have to be copied into the notebook, and one uses `qplot()`.
- **R's own machinery** (26): thirteen are drawn, among them grobs in `annotation_custom()` and `guide_custom()`, arrowheads on a theme's lines, `%+%` and `%+replace%`, and `resolution()`. Ten are set aside: `ggproto()` objects, which Giggly doesn't have (the notebook explains what Giggly does instead), and `qplot()`, which ggplot2 has deprecated. Three still wait on plotmath.
- **Everything else** (10): nine are drawn. The last one names colors by R's list of 657, and Giggly uses CSS's.
- **Maps** (32): still waiting, every one of them.

## What's left

Of the 76 examples Giggly doesn't draw, 41 are set aside on purpose:

- **20 are about R itself** rather than charts: saving a plot to a PDF, printing plots in a loop, putting the global theme back, building and printing a mapping or a `ggproto` object. Giggly draws SVG only and has no global settings or objects like those, so these notebooks explain how Giggly does it and move on.
- **12 use `qplot()` or `annotation_logticks()`**, which ggplot2 itself has deprecated or replaced.
- **5 need R's random numbers**, up to 10,000 of them, copied into the notebook.
- **4 are choices**: the two logistic regressions, R's color names, and a custom guide that ggplot2 sizes so big it squeezes the panel.

That leaves 35 waiting.

Three use plotmath, R's little language for writing formulas in labels. Giggly's answer to that is [gig_typst](https://hex.pm/packages/gig_typst), which sets titles, labels and annotations in Typst, maths included. The notebooks don't draw with it yet, so those three are still on the list.

The other 32 are maps. In R these lean on the `maps` and `sf` packages, which bring map data, geometries and coordinate reference systems. That's a whole geospatial stack, and a project of its own. But it's started: as of today gig_datasets has outlines of the US states, the world and New Zealand, laid out the way ggplot2's `map_data()` lays them, ready for its next release.

So what's next? Maps. That's not taste, it's arithmetic: 32 of the 35.
